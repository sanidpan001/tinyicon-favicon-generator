Favicon Generator: How to Design an Icon That Survives at 16 Pixels

A favicon is the smallest piece of branding your website owns, and arguably the hardest-working. It sits in the browser tab, the bookmarks bar, the history list, the mobile home screen and the app switcher — often as the only visual clue a visitor has about which of their forty open tabs is yours. TinyIcon is a favicon generator built like a laboratory bench: you bring the raw material — a letter or two, an emoji, or an image you already have — and the lab renders it at every size the modern web actually requests, so you can judge the result honestly before you ship it. Nothing is uploaded, nothing is watermarked, and every size can be downloaded on its own.

The central problem of favicon design is scale. A logo that looks magnificent on a billboard becomes grey soup at sixteen pixels. At that size you have a grid of 256 dots in total, fewer than a single character of the text you are reading now. Fine lines vanish, gradients muddy, and detailed illustrations collapse into unrecognisable blobs. Professional icon designers solve this by designing for the smallest size first and treating the large sizes as a bonus. TinyIcon encourages exactly that workflow: the preview wall shows the true pixel sizes side by side, and the browser-test strip holds your icon at a genuine 16 pixels next to the big hero render, so wishful thinking is impossible. If the tiny tile does not read instantly, the design — not the renderer — needs to change.

Three ways to build an icon

Text mode is the workhorse. One or two bold letters — your initials, the first letter of your product, a memorable abbreviation — set on a strong background colour remain legible at almost any size. TinyIcon automatically scales the type down as you add characters, because three or four letters in a sixteen-pixel box demand restraint. Monograms are the oldest form of favicon for a reason: type foundries have spent centuries making letterforms that survive small sizes, and borrowing that craft is smarter than fighting it. Pair a distinctive background colour with a contrasting letter colour and you have an icon that reads as a brand mark even before anyone can quite make out the letter.

Emoji mode is the fastest path to personality. A single emoji — a rocket, a wave, a leaf, a slice of pizza — carries colour, shape and meaning in one glyph, and modern operating systems render emoji beautifully at icon sizes. Emoji favicons suit side projects, documentation sites, event pages and personal blogs, anywhere a playful tone is an asset. Because emoji already have transparent backgrounds and strong silhouettes, they pair well with a soft rounded-square backdrop in a complementary colour. TinyIcon lets you type any emoji your device offers or tap a preset, then shapes the background and corners around it.

Image mode is for when you already have a logo or artwork. Drop in a PNG or JPG and TinyIcon centre-crops it to a square, applies your padding, and renders the full size range. Padding matters more than most people expect: artwork that touches the edges of the canvas looks cramped in a rounded browser tab and can lose its corners entirely when a platform masks the icon into a circle. Eight to fifteen percent padding gives the mark room to breathe and survives masking gracefully. Simple, bold, high-contrast images win; busy photographs rarely survive the journey down to sixteen pixels, and the preview wall will tell you so within seconds.

Which sizes do you actually need?

The size list in TinyIcon is not arbitrary — each entry answers a real request from a real platform. Sixteen and thirty-two pixels serve classic desktop browser tabs and bookmarks, with the larger one covering high-density retina displays. Forty-eight pixels appears in Windows shortcuts and some search results. The 180-pixel size is the Apple touch icon, shown when an iPhone or iPad user saves your site to their home screen. Android and progressive web apps ask for 192 and 512 pixels in the web app manifest, and the 512 also doubles as a splash and store icon. The 24, 64, 128 and 256 sizes fill in browser UI, taskbars and high-resolution contexts in between. Generating the full ladder from one master design keeps every context consistent, instead of the patchwork that results from resizing files by hand over the years.

Alongside the PNG set, TinyIcon builds a classic favicon.ico containing the 16, 32 and 48 pixel renders in a single file. The ICO format dates from the earliest days of the web, yet browsers still request /favicon.ico by default when a page does not declare icons, and some older tools and enterprise environments handle nothing else. Shipping both — a multi-size ICO for the traditionalists and crisp PNGs declared explicitly in your HTML — covers essentially every client that will ever ask.

Backgrounds, corners and the shape of modern icons

Icons no longer live as bare squares. Android masks icons into circles and squircles, browsers round tab icons subtly, and operating systems place them on shelves, docks and home screens with wildly different wallpapers behind them. The corner-radius control in TinyIcon exists so you can decide how your icon behaves under all that masking: a full fifty percent radius makes a true circle that no mask can damage, a gentle twenty percent looks like a contemporary app tile, and a sharp square keeps a technical, editorial character. A solid background colour is usually safer than transparency for a main favicon, because a transparent icon's letterforms can disappear against a dark browser theme or a busy wallpaper. If you do choose transparency — useful for overlay-style marks — test the preview tiles against both light and dark surroundings before committing.

Colour choice follows the usual rules of contrast, compressed. At sixteen pixels, your eye reads value — light versus dark — long before it reads hue. A mint background with near-black letters, like the TinyIcon default, works because the luminance gap is enormous. Pastel-on-white and dark-on-dark combinations fail in tabs even when they look refined on a brand board. If your brand colour is pale, consider using it as the letter colour on a deep charcoal field instead, or deepen the background one shade for the favicon only. Small deviations from the brand palette at favicon size are standard professional practice; recognisability beats pedantry.

Putting the files on your site

Once you have downloaded the sizes you need, place them in your site's root or an assets folder and reference them from the head of your HTML. A shortcut-icon link points legacy browsers at the ICO file, standard icon links declare the 16 and 32 pixel PNGs, the apple-touch-icon link serves the 180, and the manifest icons cover Android and installable web apps. Browsers cache favicons aggressively — famously so — which is why a redesigned icon can seem to refuse to update. Adding a version query string to the file name, or bumping the file name itself, forces a fresh fetch when you relaunch. Search engines also display favicons beside results on mobile, and they favour sites whose icon is crisp, square and at least 48 pixels in the declared set, one more reason to generate the full ladder rather than a single file.

Finally, treat the favicon as a living part of the brand. When you redesign, redesign the smallest size first and let the large render follow. Test in an actual browser tab next to the sites your visitors also keep open, because that crowded shelf — not the design file — is where your icon must win. With live previews at every size, per-size PNG downloads and a proper ICO export, TinyIcon turns that whole professional workflow into about thirty seconds of play. Pick a letter, an emoji or an image, shape it, check the tiny tiles, and ship an icon you can be proud of at sixteen pixels and beyond.

Frequently asked questions

What sizes should a modern favicon set include?

A practical modern set covers 16×16 and 32×32 for browser tabs, 48×48 for shortcuts, 180×180 for Apple touch icons, 192×192 and 512×512 for Android and progressive web apps, plus a classic multi-size .ico file for older browsers. TinyIcon renders all of these from one design.

Can I make a favicon from an emoji?

Yes. Switch TinyIcon to Emoji mode, pick one of the preset emoji or type your own, choose a background colour and corner style, and every size is rendered instantly. Emoji favicons are a fast way to give a small project a friendly, recognisable identity.

What image format works best for the image mode?

A square PNG or JPG with a simple, high-contrast subject works best. TinyIcon centre-crops your image to a square, applies your chosen padding, background and corners, and exports clean PNG files. Very detailed photos shrink poorly at 16 pixels, so bold shapes and logos give the clearest results.

Why does my favicon look blurry in the browser tab?

Blurriness usually means the browser is scaling a single large image down, or the design has thin lines and low contrast that disappear at 16 pixels. Use the live 16-pixel preview in TinyIcon to check legibility, simplify the design, increase contrast, and ship the exact sizes browsers ask for instead of one oversized file.

How do I add the downloaded favicon to my website?

Upload the files to your site's root folder and add link tags in the head of your HTML: a shortcut icon link for the .ico file, icon links for the 32×32 and 16×16 PNGs, an apple-touch-icon link for the 180×180 PNG, and theme-color meta if you use the 192 and 512 sizes in a web app manifest.

Is TinyIcon free and are my images uploaded anywhere?

Yes, TinyIcon is completely free with unlimited usage, no signup and no watermark. Everything is drawn locally in your browser with the canvas API — your images, text and emoji are never uploaded to a server, so the tool works privately and even offline once the page has loaded.
